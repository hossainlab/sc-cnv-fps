Single-cell cancer studies split cells into malignant and non-malignant compartments using copy-number inference or supervised classifiers, but these methods are rarely run on tissue containing no tumor. We assembled 14 tumor-free scRNA-seq datasets (36,528 cells from 76 donor-dataset combinations; 10 healthy organs and 4 inflammatory-disease datasets) and ran inferCNV (as infercnvpy), CopyKAT and ikarus under published decision rules; every positive call is false by construction. infercnvpy and CopyKAT made false calls in every dataset and ikarus in 12 of 14: 0.5-19.3% for a two-criterion rule adapted from Tirosh and colleagues, 4.3-56.2% for a forced two-group split with an immune reference and up to 78.0% without one, 3.0-63.0% for CopyKAT and 0-100% for ikarus, with CopyKAT and ikarus each calling about 31% of cells pooled. In-silico positive controls showed per-cell discrimination near chance for single arm events (AUC 0.53), rising to 0.85 at 18 events. At a false-call rate matched on the same diploid cells used to measure it, so that the factor is an upper bound, the continuous score detected 2.2-5.3 times as many altered cells as the published rules in all eight estimable rule comparisons, and up to 10.3 times as many as an external classifier. False calls share a pseudo-karyotype that recurs across tissues (median Spearman rho = 0.54) and tracks local gene density (rho = -0.53, P < 10^-4 against a within-chromosome circular-shift null): gene-dense regions look deleted. A cell-type-conditional empirical null with Benjamini-Hochberg control at alpha = 5% cut the per-cell false-call rate to a median of 0.03% in 13 of 14 datasets (28 false calls among 33,536 cells), although 7 of these 13 still received at least one call, so extreme-tail control was not exact, and sensitivity was not evaluated. In psoriatic skin from a separate study, a null from other tissues failed (26.8%); in a separate donor-held-out evaluation the corresponding cross-tissue null called 27.1% of cells while a null from held-out donors of the same study called 0.03%.
